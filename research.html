Research Interests

The two questions I'm focused on answering right now belong to two distinct domains. I don't include areas I have not explicitly done research in, but otherwise operating systems, file system management, and programming languages would definitely be on this list! 1. Can principled membership inference attacks expose security risks in sequenced health record data? 2.   • Theory and Practice of Parallel Algorithms. I have done work mainly in the empirical side of parallel algorithms, but one of my goals as a researcher is to substantiate the algorithms I develop with principled reasoning about their efficiency. What drew me in to this subject to begin with is the many complications of mutual data access, and the unique methods that can be applied to parallelize seemingly sequential algorithms. Take prime sieve, for example; the two main ways to make the algorithm parallel are to have single cores handle checking specific sets of numbers (blocking) by checking where the first multiple of each known prime lands in each block OR you can simply have each core be assigned to a set of primes and cross off all their multiples. Both work in theory, but the first is much better and cleaner in practice due to, if not the advantage of no locking, its cache locality.
  • Data Privacy. Membership Inference Attacks against machine learning models exploit one of the the underlying necessities of learning, viz. memorization. We know that in practice, almost all reasonably useful ML models perform differently on their training/test sets, and we can leverage this to determine whether an arbitrary sample was used for training. This presents clear privacy risks with sensitive data such as medical images, though there are ways to mitigate the risk with some performance penalties (e.g. DP-training). One question we might ask ourselves, then, is whether there exists any substantial risk in these deployments; if not, then there's no reason to incur such a penalty. Specifically, I'm working on this question with respect to sequence data, which requires some adjustments to be made to the existing literature attacks.
